Fall Back, Fall Back! (A Slight Pivot)

July 9, 2026 • 4014 words • 19 min
career raspberrypi arduino cybersecurity hacking

I have a confession to make: I haven’t been feeling very good about my career path for a while now. Despite my intentions to use a traditional IT route to get into a semi-traditional sector of cybersecurity, I was really not enjoying the idea of working helpdesk. Beyond that, I wasn’t very confident that I’d ever make it to what I actually wanted to do.

I’ve never enjoyed social interactions with strangers; I can do it, I’m not socially inept, but it’s certainly not my favorite thing to do. Tie to that the fact that I’ve obviously heard all the horror stories from helpdesk employees dealing with difficult customers and spiking their stress levels in the process, which is something I certainly don’t need more of.

Yet, I kept quiet to avoid killing my chances at getting hired, and I continued pursuing that path simply because it seemed like I didn’t have any other options. I don’t have any direct IT work experience. At the same time, I have a skill set beyond what most folks in entry-level helpdesk have. Even so, right now, I lack the space to set up my TenshiNet home lab in earnest (though I have plenty of hardware to work with) so it’s hard to get resume-worthy practical projects.

All the while, I applied to hundreds of jobs like that because I felt I had no other real path to take… and that led to much disappointment, as I only got a single interview from all those applications and I still didn’t get the job. This repeated process destroyed my confidence, left me wondering if I’d ever get my chance or if I’d end up working dead-end jobs I don’t enjoy for the rest of my life and never come close to reaching a career that I can both enjoy and truly take pride in.

Breaking insanity

As people say, the definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over again expecting different results. Something had to give. My real passion certainly wasn’t in talking to strangers on the phone for eight hours a day, resetting passwords and repeating troubleshooting platitudes with a sprinkling of occasional difficult clients set to ruin my day.

No, my passion lies more in tinkering with computers, learning new tech, getting down to a baser level of how the tech works, and finding fun or creative solutions to limitations I encounter. This is why I find penetration testing (and even security engineering) far more interesting than SOC analyst or GRC work, which were both cybersecurity sub-fields that I initially started exploring.

At the same time, I struggle with things like advanced math, physics and so forth. My mind isn’t a computer, it doesn’t work in numbers and calculations. My background is more on the creative side of things: game development, creative writing, that sort of thing. My mind works in scenery and story, not cold numbers and abstract logic. On top of that, I have no interest in attending a college and couldn’t afford to go to one even if I wanted to. All of that considered, pivoting toward a computer science or electrical engineering degree simply would not be the right path for me to take.

Enter: my recent interest in microcontrollers and single-board computers.

When a hobby seems unrelated

I’ve been learning Arduino and Raspberry Pi over the past year, but purely at a hobbyist level, with a ‘whenever I get to it’ pacing. My uncle bought me an Arduino starter kit years ago but I never got around to it, primarily because I saw “some soldering required” and assumed I wasn’t equipped for it, because I didn’t have a soldering iron at the time.

However, after I actually learned what a breadboard was, I realized the soldering was mostly just for actually assembling a project beyond the prototypes. This opened the whole world of Arduino up to me, even though the starter kit I had was really basic, consisting only of LEDs, resistors and buttons to go along with the Uno, breadboard and jumper wires.

I didn’t really get into Arduino for the sake of learning coding, or to make any specific projects: I got into it because I like tinkering and, I don’t know, I just find something very appealing about holding a sheet of silicon and soldered components in my hand. Even the swappable DIP-style microcontroller chip on the Uno R3 is appealing for me to look at, with my hobbyist interest in retro computers.

From Arduino, I got into Raspberry Pi thanks to a gift of a Pi Model B (first gen). It couldn’t do a whole lot due to its age but I did manage to flash it with PiOS Lite and set up Pi-Hole to filter out ads, trackers and malware at the DNS level. I documented this project on an earlier post.

That intrigued me so much that I decided to shell out some decent money to get myself kits for the Pi 4, Zero 2 W and Pico 2 W. I wanted to be able to not only learn each of those major form factors but I also had different goals in mind for each: the Zero 2 W was for a DOS PC-inspired retro box for using old DOS software and playing games from my early childhood. I also knew I wanted to learn the Pico, since I was enjoying Arduino so much and I knew it was also a microcontroller running a form of Python, which I was already casually learning as a side skill. As for the Pi 4, I have a project in mind for it but I’ll talk about that at a later time, as it’s the most relevant to this new career path I’m striking out on.

I started these as hobbies, more as a means of getting my mind off of the stress of my career issues. What I didn’t expect was to gain a greater understanding of how computers and electronics work, nor did I expect to find myself understanding coding significantly more than my previous attempts to get into it…

But that’s what happened! The first time I noticed this difference was when I was watching a video by The 8-Bit Guy where David was talking about how retro games controllers worked. Before he fully explained certain things, I found myself immediately making connections in my head with a “so that’s how it works” fascination. That’s when it clicked: I wasn’t just having fun with a side hobby, I was actually learning very practical and real-world applications to what I was doing. I found myself starting to think about how electronics, even simple things like a desktop fan, run a circuit and interact with their components.

The next time I noticed this difference was when I started working with the Pico.

For those who don’t know much (if anything) about Arduino, you code your projects using a modified version of C++ by default. I have a limited amount of experience with JavaScript (Sphere and basic web dev) and C# (Unity) due to previous projects from my late teens and early adult years, and those languages have a C-like syntax, so Arduino’s scripting language isn’t challenging for me to understand the basics of. Even so, I’m not really learning any classic C-based language in any serious capacity, at least not for now.

On the flip side, Python is a language I’m learning at a more serious capacity. It has a lot of application to cybersecurity, AI, data science and virtually every other facet of IT, so it was mostly a practicality thing more than anything, as I don’t find its syntax that much easier to grasp than C-style languages. Initially, I took up a course; I’ve gone slower with it than intended, but I do enjoy it; I even wrote my first original script with Python, an automation tool for background updating of software on Windows! Even so, I found certain parts more challenging than others. Fortunately for me, the Pi Pico is typically used with MicroPython, an implementation of Python that runs directly on the Pico itself.

When I started working with the Pico, I quickly noticed that concepts that were previously feeling abstract to me were suddenly ‘clicking’ and making much more sense. I’ve always been a hands-on learner more than anything. Seeing physical results of code I wrote, results that interact with physical components, that made things come together in my mind much easier than just seeing input or results in a terminal window. To my understanding, this was actually the primary goal of the Raspberry Pi project: to help teach people how computers work at a lower level and to learn how to use that to their advantage when learning how to code.

A new inflection point

The problem I saw was that, aside from developing a better understanding of Python and potentially setting up security-related projects, this hobby would remain just that: a hobby for fun, to get my mind off of the problems I was facing. However, following a conversation with my uncle (the same one who gifted the Arduino kit) on Mother’s Day, I started thinking, “What if I can find a way to integrate this so that it becomes an intrinsic part of my career?”

Like I said, I knew I didn’t want to go into electrical engineering engineering for a few reasons, and I really do not like math, so I wasn’t sure if it would be possible for me to integrate a hobbyist interest into a career skill set. I started researching, and I ran my career situation through AI to give me another perspective on the whole thing. Together, this helped me realize a few things:

  1. My pursuit of helpdesk wasn’t helping anyone, least of all myself. I was willing to do it purely because I thought that was the only viable way to get my foot in the door, but it was a misuse of my interests and abilities.
  2. There are entry-level fields related to technical support and quality assurance testing that would let me use, interact with, and learn more about SBCs/microcontrollers.
  3. Taking this to an application within security, I could pursue embedded and IoT security, and this would be a particularly fun way to approach hacking.

I genuinely can’t figure out why I didn’t make the connection on point #3. I was aware of the usage of microcontrollers and SBCs within infrastructure, IoT devices and the like but for whatever reason, I never made the connection that I could pursue that and naturally synergize a career goal with my latest hobby.

Much like my understanding of coding, computers and electronics started coming together thanks to Arduino/Raspberry Pi, these career-related questions and thoughts started coming together after I made that connection and learned about how the entry-level positions may change. It was very much a revelatory moment for me and immediately renewed a lot of hope I had lost over the past three years of moving toward this career change.

Some reasons this might work

As I thought about this whole switch more, the connections and reasons just kept flowing. That’s what made me so certain that this is the right path for me to venture onto.

  1. First off, embedded systems are critical to infrastructure across the developed world. The amount of microcontrollers and SBCs that exist in virtually every facet of infrastructure is insane to think about. While they make a more advanced daily life possible, they also present a very real security risk, with society potentially headed toward Watch Dogs-style attacks on the regular.

  2. This segment of cybersecurity seems far less “desired” by other people who are entering this industry. It could just be that I’m not seeing as many people talking about it, but it seems rare for me to find people talking about interest in entering embedded systems security; most people seem to just want to hack websites or use a SIEM all day. Less interest = less competition = more opportunity for me.

  3. Unlike helpdesk, the entry-level roles in this path appear to be a bit more friendly to those who have hands-on technical projects rather than certifications or the need to prove abstract knowledge somehow. In other words, me tinkering with a Raspberry Pi and doing something cool with it is literally something I can put on my resume above previous work experience, all while having fun with it and acquiring even more knowledge/understanding of the topic.

  4. For better and worse, IoT is the future. Eventually, the IoT security space will be one of the most important sectors of cybersecurity, especially as AI gets integrated into more and more of these systems. This presents significantly more opportunity for me in the future, especially when viewed parallel with point #2.

  5. Finally, as a privacy advocate, I could theoretically start a business down the road which develops open source, privacy-respecting IoT devices and similar technology. Of course, I’m talking many years from now, but still: it could present some really cool potential down the road! Unlike my previous paths, this is one where I see a very clear opportunity years from now to make a difference in something I care very much about.

There’s also a bonus reason, albeit one that’s much less important: I mentioned Watch Dogs in point #1. That’s one of my favorite game franchises. The concept is that you’re playing as a gray hat hacker (good motivations/morals but operating outside of the law) and you use “smart” tech as your hacking playground. You’re fighting fire with fire: combatting surveillance with surveillance, while educating the masses on the dangers of not caring about privacy. I always found that idea intriguing, and while I would never become a gray or black hat (I like my freedom), being able to attack devices much like Aiden Pearce or Marcus Holloway would be a truly fun way to make a living.

A tactical retreat

So, here’s my plan of action.

First off, I’ve been trying to work on two early cybersecurity certifications: CompTIA’s Security+ and TryHackMe’s SEC1. I have deadlines for both of them by the end of this year and trying to balance that with further cybersecurity learning and constantly stressing over trying to find a job, it was just becoming too much to handle all at once.

Therefore, I ordered a tactical retreat in my life. I paused my job search for the next few months and will resume that part sometime after the summer is over.

This is important for a handful of reasons. The most notable is that I need more time to really dig into my new hobby and start making resume-worthy projects. I also need to get back to working on TryHackMe with more fervor; I want to finish the Jr Penetration Tester learning path so that my next certs can be a step up toward their PT1 cert and probably the CEH after that, provided things go as planned.

There’s also the fact that taking this time of reduced pressure will be better for my mental health, giving me time to have fun while I learn, rather than trying to race against an arbitrary clock. Thankfully, I do have the luxury of taking this extra bit of time, though I acknowledge many won’t have that luxury, so I can’t say this retreat plan would work for everyone.

Once I’ve learned a lot more about both Arduino and the Pico, once I’ve finished a few more projects, once I’ve gotten my base level certs and come close to finishing that learning path, I will return to applying for jobs but with a new focus on technical support or QA roles related to SBCs and microcontrollers. I’m definitely more interested in the latter as I’d be getting my hands on tech to play around with in the process of testing, but if there’s one luxury I don’t have, it’s the ability to be overly picky.

Fortunately, I did also discover a job possibility in my local area if I can’t find anything remote in this field. This local job wouldn’t be exactly what I had in mind but it is related, and two friends have worked there; both say it’s a solid option, so that gives me a relatively good fallback/safety net if things don’t go quite as planned.

Long-term strategy

Aside from the immediate plan of action, I have loose goals but I’ve been learning that making rigid, unmoving goals isn’t exactly the best idea when it comes to career changes. The obvious is my long-term goal of progressing into embedded systems/IoT security, specifically penetration testing. I’ve been morphing my homelab and future lab plans to meet this goal, including a slow build-up of DIY IoT plans as future Arduino/Pi projects. Beyond that, I’m leaving it up to God because “A man’s heart plans his way, but the Lord directs his steps.”

I’m also planning to switch one of my laptops over to Parrot OS to use as my mobile hacking system. Currently, I just run Kali within a virtual machine on my primary PC, as well as the light installation present on my only Windows laptop via WSL. Those suffice for web or software pentesting but if I want to do hardware-related pentesting, I need a bare metal system running something like Kali. I opted for Parrot instead, as they have a daily usage version in parallel with the hacking version, which I’d prefer so that I’m not limited in what I can use this specific laptop for.

I also mentioned business ideas. This is extremely rudimentary, I’m playing it by ear, but I’ve been coming up with business ideas in this space. One of my previous career attempts was entrepreneurship; I failed at that career because all of my business ideas were just too big for my current budget. For the life of me, I couldn’t come up with something that I could accomplish even with bootstrapping, and I didn’t really have any appropriate contacts for the ideas either.

That’s not the case with these business ideas; because of the DIY nature of SBCs and microcontrollers, a lot of the fragments of ideas I have right now could be a lot more feasible, especially after I make more and more contacts. Inspired by Paul McWhorter’s “Eldorado Space Program” concept, one of these ideas involves a “near space” program with high-altitude balloons, data collection, partnerships with educational institutions, multimedia coverage, etc. Obviously, I don’t have the budget for that one either… right now. It’s still a fun idea. The point I’m making is that the business ideas I have now are significantly more achievable after I’ve built experience and my professional network.

Some thoughts

Part of my goal with this blog has been to help other people realize they’re not alone when facing the currently harsh state of the cybersecurity and wider IT industries. I’d like to leave a couple thoughts for the people who read this blog and struggle with feeling hopeless in pursuit of the IT/cybersecurity career path.

If you can, find a niche.

Early IT training covers the gamut of subjects, and that’s absolutely a good thing: you need the foundational skills no matter where you end up. However, you can’t eternally learn everything. It’s not possible and it just causes you to stress out or lose confidence when you realize that you’ll inevitably have weak spots.

Finding your niche, the spot where you can truly say you love what you’re doing, that’s the key. Easier said than done, for sure, but it’s crucial. Sometimes you do have to bite the bullet and do things in your career that you don’t enjoy, but if you find yourself dreading every aspect of your current path, try to find a different path. Before you consider taking a path outside of IT/infosec, do all that you can to find out if there’s a niche for you to dig into. You don’t want to waste all the time, effort and money you put into your career path unless you have no other choice. On that subject…

If you’re still struggling to find your niche or find a way to pursue this career path, take a break.

I don’t mean a temporary break from pursuing this career path, I mean take a break from the path entirely.

As I showed in this post, sometimes a tactical retreat is necessary to pivot onto the road to victory. If you’re a career changer, leverage your existing experience in your previous career and look for temporary jobs, contract work, maybe even freelancing. That way, you’ll be earning some income but you’re not locked in the way you would if you were going back to that previous career in general.

This gives you two important resources: time and money, while also avoiding large resume gaps.

It buys you time because you’re not working permanently or full-time, meaning you’ll still have the ability to study and build up your IT and security knowledge.

If you can, find some fun homelab projects to work on. Raspberry Pi can be helpful with all sectors of IT, not just the niche of embedded/IoT security. Whether that’s as simple as setting up Pi-Hole, building a honeypot, setting up a mobile Kali hack station, setting up a media server with Jellyfin, I consider Raspberry Pi to be a must-try for all fields of cybersecurity (except, perhaps, GRC). It will not only teach you a lot, it will be hands-on experience for your resume and it presents a world of practical, daily upsides on top of it all!

Finally, I’d encourage you to make another realization that I’ve made:

It simply is not possible for people to be a machine. Stop trying to outdo AI. Master your skills and master yourself.

For three and a half years, I spent all of my energy and much of my time trying to effectively become a machine, mastering everything with total efficiency. You can’t do that. Nobody is good at everything, and that includes anything in tech. Do you really think that every senior network engineer is going to be able to build robots with Arduino? Or do you think that every electrical engineer is going to know how to configure a Linux server? No. Sure, there are plenty of people who dabble in more than one field of study, and I think that’s great: it helped me learn where my niche is.

However, it can also lead a person to feel very discouraged when they realize that they have a weak spot that they can’t find the time, desire or understanding to improve to a professional grade. From my own experience, until I came to understand this, I constantly felt under-equipped and insufficient.

I know, it’s not easy to resist this temptation: job openings list ridiculous amounts of credentials and experience as requirements, but unless you’re a long-standing pro with tier-one skill mastery, those companies likely won’t even give you the time of day. That’s why practical projects are so crucial; again, this goes back to the sheer value that getting into Raspberry Pi can offer you.

Bonus tip: try to get your resume onto a single page. Until recently, I wasn’t sure how to do this. Most tips online seemed to only make my resume end up longer, not shorter. Once again, AI came to the rescue here, giving me a template that I used to craft a better resume, one that prioritizes hands-on projects, skills, brevity (ironic, considering this post’s word count) and common keywords for the jobs I’m aiming for. I now have my resume on a single page, but I don’t feel like I lost much room to “sell myself” to potential employers.

Sometimes, all you need to get out of a rut is to change your perspective. It may seem like a brick wall in front of you but if you’re focused on that, you might miss the window just around the corner.

Stand your ground and keep your eyes open!
